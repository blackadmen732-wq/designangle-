The second finding is the expensive one. The phone number on a trades site is a 21 pixel tall tap target — the most valuable thing on the page, at half the height a thumb needs.

An icon is light on a shape, not a rounded square.

The shape people recognise from a home screen is a superellipse. Its curvature never stops changing, so the straight edge eases into the corner rather than meeting an arc at a seam. No border-radius does that. This one is |x|⁵ + |y|⁵ = 1, sampled at 200 points, emitted as an SVG path, and it deviates from the true curve by at most 0.00005.

border-radius: 23.5% — four arcs joined to four straight edges.
The superellipse — wider at the shoulder, tighter at the apex of the corner.
Overlaid, because side by side at this size the two look the same. The gap at the shoulder is the whole difference, and it is what reads as "drawn by someone" without being nameable.
01

The shape

Flat fill. Nothing here says which way is up.

02

Rim

A stroke on the path itself, so it follows the contour into the corners. Bright where the light lands, with a weaker bounce returning on the far edge.

03

Body and arc

A radial gradient offset toward the light, and one wide ellipse rising from below, clipped by the shape. One arc, not two.

04

Lit glyph

The glyph takes the same light and drops a shadow onto the plate, so it sits on the surface instead of being painted on it.

Three of those were wrong in the first version. The rim was a rectangular mask the clip cut away, leaving 67 pixels of bare corner on a 302 pixel mark. The specular arc was positioned so far below the box that it painted three levels of grey into the bottom two pixels and nothing else. And the shadow did not exist at all, because clip-path clips an element's own box-shadow. The shadow is on a wrapper now.

The half of review that is arithmetic.

A vision model cannot divide. Asking one whether 4.31 clears 4.5 spends it on a job a calculator does better, and it will sometimes say yes.

01

Contrast against the real background

Not the declared colour. The one actually painted, flattened through every translucent layer above it.

02

Gradient text, by its worst stop

Clipped background text computes to transparent. The stops are pulled from the computed value and the darkest is graded.

03

The box, not the icon

44px, counting a hit area grown by a pseudo-element. The drawn size stays what it was drawn.

04

Transitions that re-run layout

Width, height, top, gap. Paint-only properties are allowed, briefly: the rule's reason is dropped frames, not colour.

05

Inputs below 16px

iOS zooms on focus and the layout jumps under a thumb already moving toward the field.

06

Its own blind spots

Where a stylesheet cannot be read, it says so rather than reporting a pass. A checker that blames the page for what it failed to see is worse than none.

What is true on a phone is not true on a page.

Most kits carry one list and hope. These are kept apart, and each rule says why it does not cross over.

Phone

  • 44px targets, because a finger pad is far wider than a pointer and lands less precisely
  • Actions in the bottom third, where the thumb already rests
  • Inputs at 16px or iOS zooms on focus
  • Safe-area insets for the notch and the home indicator
  • Full-bleed media with inset text, which is most of what reads as native

Web

  • Hover states, which do not exist on a touch screen
  • Line-length caps, because the device is not the constraint
  • Breakpoints, because a page is every width and an app screen is one
  • Scroll-linked motion, which fights a phone's own scrolling
  • A fold people scroll past by habit

The split is sparse against dense, not dark against light. That was wrong when it was first written here, and a reference redesign disproved it: a dark map screen can carry more at once than a white dashboard.
