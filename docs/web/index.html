The craft

An icon is four layers of light, not a rounded square.

The shape people recognise from a home screen is a superellipse — its curvature is continuous, so the straight edge eases into the corner instead of meeting an arc at a visible seam. No border-radius does that. This one is an SVG path used as a clip, and the depth on top of it is three more layers, in this order.

01

The shape

A clipped superellipse. Flat fill, nothing else yet.

02

Flat fill

The brand colour, evenly lit. Nothing here says which way is up.

03

Rim light

A 1.6px inner edge, bright where the light lands and gone by the opposite corner. This alone reads as glass.

04

Body gradient and arc

A radial gradient offset toward the light, plus one wide ellipse rising from below. One, not two.

border-radius: 23.5%

Four arcs joined to four straight edges. The seam is small, and once you see it you cannot stop.

Clipped superellipse

Curvature that never stops changing. This is the difference people read as "made by someone", without being able to name it.

With the three light layers

Same shape. The depth is entirely gradient and clip — there is no image, no blur filter, and no 3D.

What it caught

The accent had been shipping at 4.31 to 1.

Not a hypothetical. #e03a4b was the brand colour on a live restaurant site, used for the eyebrow label and the form's error text. It fails the 4.5 to 1 rule the kit itself sets, and it had been failing the whole time, because nothing was checking.

The replacement was not picked by eye. contrast.js holds hue and saturation and walks lightness down until it passes everywhere the colour is used — so a client's brand red stays their red, only deep enough to read.

That second finding was the expensive one. The phone number on a trades site is a 21 pixel tall tap target — the single most valuable thing on the page, at half the size a thumb needs.

What runs

The half of review that is arithmetic.

A vision model cannot divide. Asking one whether 4.31 passes 4.5 wastes it on a job a calculator does better. These are measured first; judgement is spent on whether the thing looks good.

01

Contrast against the real background

Not the declared colour — the one actually painted, flattened through every translucent layer above it.

02

The box, not the icon

44px minimum, counting a hit area grown by a pseudo-element. The look stays the size it was drawn.

03

Transitions that re-run layout

Width, height, top, gap. Paint-only properties are allowed, briefly — the rule's reason is frame drops, not colour.

04

Inputs below 16px

iOS zooms the page on focus and the layout jumps under the thumb already moving toward the field.

05

Sideways overflow

At 400 and at 1280. Several faults here appeared at exactly one of the two.

06

Its own blind spots

When a stylesheet cannot be read, it says could not verify. A tool that blames the page for what it failed to see is worse than none.

Two sets of rules

What is true on a phone is not true on a page.

Most kits carry one list and pretend. These are kept apart, and each rule says why it does not cross over.

Phone

  • 44px targets, because a thumb is 10mm across and arrives roughly
  • Actions in the bottom third, where the thumb already is
  • Inputs at 16px or iOS zooms on focus
  • Safe-area insets for the notch and the home indicator
  • Full-bleed media, text inset — most of what reads as native

Web

  • Hover states, which do not exist on a touch screen
  • Line-length caps, because the device is not the constraint
  • Breakpoints across every width from 320 to 2560
  • Scroll-linked motion, which fights a phone's own scrolling
  • A fold people scroll past by habit

The split is sparse against dense, not dark against light. That was wrong when it was written, and a reference redesign disproved it: a dark map screen can be denser than a white dashboard.
